1Introduction

The Indus Valley Civilization (IVC), also called the Harappan Civilization, was one of the earliest urban civilizations of the Indian subcontinent. It flourished during the Bronze Age and is especially known for planned cities, drainage, craft production, trade and standardized weights.

Harappan Civilization

Named after Harappa, one of the first major sites excavated.

Bronze Age

Bronze was used, especially an alloy of copper and tin. Iron was not in regular use.

Urban character

Planned streets, baked-brick buildings, wells and covered drains.

Exam reminder: “Harappan” is the archaeological name derived from Harappa; “Indus Valley” is the traditional geographical name.

2Chronological Timeline

Early Harappan
c. 3300–2600 BCE
  • Growth of villages and regional settlements.
  • Expansion of agriculture, crafts and exchange.
  • Gradual emergence of urban features.
Mature Harappan
c. 2600–1900 BCE
  • Peak of urban civilization.
  • Planned cities, drainage and standardized weights.
  • Long-distance trade and extensive craft production.
Late Harappan
c. 1900–1300 BCE
  • Decline of major urban centres.
  • Changes in settlement patterns and reduced long-distance exchange.
  • Regional cultures and smaller settlements became more prominent.

Dates are conventional archaeological ranges. Regional transitions differed; the late phase continued beyond 1700 BCE in some areas.

3Geographical Extent

The civilization extended across present-day Pakistan and parts of north-western and western India, with some settlements reaching Afghanistan. Its commonly cited extent is about 1.3 million km².

4Important Archaeological Sites

Learn each site with its present-day location, river or region, and its most testable feature.

Harappa
Ravi River; Punjab, Pakistan. Excavated by Daya Ram Sahni (1921). Cemetery H is associated with its later occupation; large granary structures are traditionally associated with the site.
Mohenjo-daro
Indus River; Sindh, Pakistan. Excavated by R. D. Banerji (1922). Great Bath, bronze Dancing Girl, Priest-King sculpture and Pashupati seal.
Chanhudaro
Sindh, Pakistan. Important bead-making and craft-production centre; no separately identified citadel.
Kalibangan
Ghaggar region; Rajasthan, India. Evidence of a ploughed field and fire altars.
Lothal
Gujarat, India; associated with the Bhogavo River. Famous for a dockyard-like structure, bead-making and trade.
Dholavira
Khadir Bet, Gujarat, India. Reservoirs, water conservation, three-part city layout and large signboard; UNESCO inscription in 2021.
Rakhigarhi
Haryana, India. One of the largest known Harappan settlements; evidence of burials, crafts and urban planning.
Banawali
Haryana, India. Evidence of pre-Harappan and Harappan phases.
Surkotada
Kutch, Gujarat, India. Fortified settlement; horse-remains claims are debated.
Alamgirpur
Hindon River region; Uttar Pradesh, India. Easternmost site in the conventional geographical list.
Manda
Chenab region; Jammu, India. Northernmost site in the conventional geographical list.
Daimabad
Pravara River region; Maharashtra, India. Famous for bronze artefacts, including animal figures and a chariot-like object; southernmost major Harappan-related settlement in the conventional list.
Mehrgarh
Balochistan, Pakistan. Earlier farming settlement important for understanding the development of agriculture before the Mature Harappan phase.
Sutkagendor
Balochistan, Pakistan. Westernmost site in the conventional geographical list.

6Town Planning & Architecture

Major features

  • Grid-pattern streets in many cities.
  • Elevated citadel and lower town at several major sites.
  • Covered drainage channels and inspection points.
  • Baked-brick buildings with standardized proportions.
  • Private and public wells.
  • Bathrooms and wastewater outlets.
  • Organized residential, craft and public spaces.

7Script, Society, Religion & Economy

Script

  • Sign-based or pictographic-looking script.
  • Still undeciphered.
  • Inscriptions often short, found on seals and pottery.
  • Generally read right-to-left; some examples may show boustrophedon.
  • Underlying language remains unknown.

Social & cultural life

  • Ornaments of gold, silver, copper, shell and beads.
  • Toys, carts and figurines.
  • Cotton textile production.
  • Different burial practices.
  • Craft specialists and urban occupations.

Religion: cautious interpretations

  • Female figurines sometimes interpreted as mother-goddess figures.
  • Pashupati seal sometimes linked to a proto-Shiva interpretation.
  • Tree and animal symbolism.
  • Great Bath possibly associated with ritual bathing.

These interpretations are debated; no complete religious system has been conclusively established.

Agriculture & animals

  • Crops: wheat, barley, peas, sesame, mustard, dates and cotton.
  • Rice evidence occurs at some sites and varies regionally.
  • Animals: cattle, buffalo, sheep, goats, pigs and dogs.
  • Humped bull appears frequently in Harappan imagery.

Metals and crafts

Trade & commerce

  • Internal exchange among settlements.
  • External contacts with Mesopotamia, Oman and Persian Gulf regions.
  • Mesopotamian texts mention Meluhha, widely associated with the Indus region.
  • Seals and standardized cubical weights supported commercial activity.
  • Lothal is associated with maritime trade.

9Decline of the Civilization

The Mature Harappan urban phase declined after about 1900 BCE. There is no single universally accepted explanation.

Climate change

Reduced rainfall and changing environmental conditions.

River changes

Shifts in river courses and water availability.

Flooding

Repeated floods affected some settlements.

Trade decline

Reduced long-distance exchange and changing economic networks.

Settlement change

Urban centres fragmented; populations shifted toward smaller settlements.

Exam point: Modern explanations generally emphasize multiple regional and environmental factors, not one sudden invasion or a single catastrophe.
